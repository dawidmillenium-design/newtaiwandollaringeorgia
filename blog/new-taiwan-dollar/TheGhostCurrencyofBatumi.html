The Ghost Currency of Batumi — Full Case File

The ghost currency of Batumi: How a 1 GEL coffee in the Alliance Palace created a phantom banking code, version-controlled live via GitHub.

The Black Sea air in Batumi hits you a certain way after a long journey. My route here was a classic traveler’s sprint: flying out from Bangkok, touching down in Istanbul for a tight, two-hour layover that barely gave me enough time to sprint between gates, and finally landing on the Georgian coast. I checked into the towering Alliance Palace, surrounded by the hum of the resort city and the rhythmic crashing of the waves against the promenade.

You would think after navigating international terminals, shifting time zones, and juggling multiple local currencies, my brain would be immune to travel anomalies. But life loves a quiet plot twist. It happened over something as mundane as a cup of morning coffee from a sleek, automated vending machine tucked away in the lobby area of the Alliance Palace.

Little did I know, that single cup of coffee was about to introduce me to a phantom currency code that doesn't exist on any central bank ledger in the world: TWN.

Act I: The Journey and the First Swipe (Erste Bank & The Phantom Code)

My wallet is a relic of modern nomadic life—a physical archive of cross-border existence. Among my cards is a Polish debit card issued by Erste Bank (rooted in the historical legacy of the Santander group). It's the card I use for European-denominated transactions, carrying it across borders like a passport stamp in plastic form.

I walked up to the vending machine in the Alliance Palace, selected a standard Americano, tapped my Erste card against the reader, and waited for the familiar satisfying beep. One Georgian lari (1 GEL). A modest price for a quick caffeine fix.

Hours later, lounging in the room with the Black Sea glistening outside the window, I opened my smartphone banking application to check my daily expenses. The notification line stopped me cold:

Płatność kartą −1,00 TWN
georgian vending group, Tbilisi · Today
Dopasowana Mastercard debit ····••••

TWN. "New Taiwan Dollar." In Georgia? My mind raced through the usual traveler's paranoia. Had my card somehow been skimmed? Was someone sitting in a cafe in Taipei sipping a latte on my dime while I was staring at the Georgian coast? But the geography didn't fit. I had just flown out of Bangkok’s Suvarnabhumi Airport weeks earlier, landed in Batumi on September 15, 2026, and the card had never left my physical possession. Furthermore, the app greeted me with my own name, matched my personal details, and pointed directly to the card sitting securely in my phone case.

I dug deeper into the bank's backend ledger statement. While the app's front-end interface panicked over a mysterious three-letter code, the hard settlement number told the true, grounded story: 1.50 PLN.

The posted złoty amount looked consistent with the coffee price. That comparison cannot by itself identify the authorization currency or the source of the TWN label.

Act II: The Control Group Experiment (Kasikorn Bank & The Reality Check)

If you are an analytical traveler, you don't just wonder about a glitch—you test it.

Curious to see if the vending machine itself was throwing a digital tantrum or if my Polish bank's parsing engine was hallucinating, I walked back down to the exact same machine a few minutes later. This time, I pulled out a completely different instrument: my Thai mobile banking card issued by Kasikorn Bank, a relic of my recent life in Bangkok.

I tapped the Kasikorn card, grabbed a second coffee, and immediately checked my smartphone notification feed.

The screen flashed: −13.44 THB

No ghosts. No phantom ISO codes. Just a clean, direct conversion: 1 GEL mapped straight to 13.44 Thai Baht.

Standing there in the lobby of the Alliance Palace holding two cups of coffee, the puzzle pieces locked into place. I suspected a currency-code mapping problem. The second card displayed THB, but I did not have the terminal or processor records needed to locate the source of "TWN."

The second app displayed a THB amount, while the first displayed TWN and a PLN posting. I would ask the issuer to verify the authorization and settlement details before attributing the difference to either bank or the terminal.

Act III: Comparing the one-lari purchase

TWN is not a valid currency code, so it has no exchange rate. These figures describe the approximate value of the one-lari coffee, not a conversion from TWN. The PLN and THB figures are reported app observations; the other estimates need a dated rate source before they can be relied on:

The takeaway: TWN has no conversion rate. In this example, the number 1 matched a one-lari receipt, but the issuer has not confirmed where the displayed code arose.

Act IV: Version Control and Repository Publishing

This site preserves my account of the transaction. Published files are not a substitute for the issuer's authorization and settlement records.

Browse the project repository and its published files on GitHub.

We put an immense amount of blind faith in the invisible machinery of modern finance: terminal → acquirer → payment network → issuing bank → smartphone app. We assume that because numbers are digital, they are infallible.

This little episode in Batumi taught me a valuable lesson. When a banking app shows an unfamiliar code or charge, save the receipt and contact the issuing bank promptly. If you suspect unauthorized use, follow its advice about locking or replacing the card. Mastercard's guidance says to contact your financial institution immediately.

The lari was real. The coffee in the Alliance Palace was warm and real. The stamps from Bangkok, Istanbul, and Batumi were stamped in honest ink. Only the three letters on the screen were hallucinating.

Verdict: So here is to the ghost currency of Batumi—may your coffee always be strong, your card limits intact, and your digital apps eventually learn the difference between an ISO country code and a cup of morning coffee.

— Dawid, Batumi, Georgia · September 2026
