The Currency That Never Existed

By Dawid Lisowski
September 29, 2026 | Batumi, Georgia

The coffee cost exactly one Georgian lari. I know this because I stood there in the fluorescent-lit lobby of Alliance Palace, watched the barista punch it into the register, and handed over my card with the casual confidence of a man who has done this a thousand times across three continents. The terminal beeped. I signed. I walked away with a paper cup sweating in the humid Batumi air, thinking about the Black Sea, about the ferry I might take to Istanbul, about nothing in particular.

Fourteen hours earlier, I had been in Bangkok.

The journey itself was a study in modern transit absurdity: a 2-hour layover in Istanbul that felt like a dream sequence—enough time to buy an overpriced simit and stare at departure boards in a language I don't speak, not enough time to remember which timezone my body occupied. I boarded the plane to Batumi with the particular fatigue of someone who has crossed too many timezones too quickly, my Polish passport (the new one, issued in Bangkok just months before) tucked into my jacket pocket, my Thai residency card buried somewhere in my carry-on.

I landed in Batumi on September 15th. The stamp in my passport says so: rectangular, grey ink, "BATUMI" and "15 09 2026" and the little airplane icon that means arrival by air. Two weeks later, on September 29th, I bought that coffee.

And that is when the financial system decided to lie to me.

The Ghost in the Machine

I checked my banking app that evening, as one does—lying in bed, the sound of Georgian traffic filtering through the window, the particular loneliness of a hotel room that isn't quite home. The Erste Bank app (Polish, formerly Santander, that peculiar institution that has followed me across borders) greeted me with its usual familiarity: "Hello, Dawid!" it said, using the name that matches my passport, my face, my existence.

Then I saw the transaction.

Płatność kartą — 1,00 TWN
georgian vending group, Tbilisi · Today

I sat up. TWN. Three letters that looked like a currency code but belonged to no currency I had ever encountered. Not in my years of travel. Not in my knowledge of international finance. Not in the ISO 4217 standard that governs such things, which I would later consult with the fervor of a man checking his own pulse.

TWN is not the New Taiwan Dollar—that would be TWD. It is not the Georgian Lari—that is GEL, the actual currency of the country where I stood. It is not anything. It is a ghost, a phantom, an unexplained label in the bank app. I cannot tell from the display where it originated.

But here is where the story turns from annoyance to fascination: the amount was correct. One lari. The coffee cost one lari. The app showed "1,00" with that European comma, and the receipt in my wallet confirmed it. The currency label was unexpected; the issuer would need to confirm the transaction data.

The Experiment

I am not a passive man. When systems malfunction, I investigate.

The next morning, I returned to the same machine. Same lobby, same fluorescent lights, same coffee selection. But this time, I used a different card: my Kasikorn Bank debit card from Thailand, the one I had kept active through years of residency in Bangkok, the one that still carried the particular plastic smell of a life I had recently left.

I bought the same coffee. One lari. The machine beeped. I waited.

My Thai banking app updated within seconds: -13.44 THB

No TWN appeared in the second app. That contrast is useful, but two card transactions do not reveal which system generated the first label.

But here is the mathematical poetry of it: if 1 GEL equals 13.44 THB, and my Polish bank had charged me 1.50 PLN for the same transaction, then the system had revealed its own internal logic. The two amounts appeared consistent with one-lari purchases. The meaning and source of the TWN field remain unconfirmed.

I had discovered the exchange rate of nothing.

The Paper Trail

I am European. We document. It is our way.

My old passport sits in a drawer, voided with a punch-hole. My replacement was issued in Bangkok in July 2026. I have withheld the document numbers from this public account.

The stamps tell the rest: Thailand departure, September 14th, Suvarnabhumi Airport, that oval blue ink that says you have left. Georgia entry, September 15th, Batumi, that rectangular grey stamp that says you have arrived.

I was here. I was legally, documentarily, physically present in Batumi when the transaction occurred. My card was in my hand. The coffee was in my other hand. Those details gave me a plausible explanation for the coffee charge, but they could not independently rule out misuse of the card.

There was an unexplained code on my banking screen.

What TWN Teaches Us

We live inside systems we cannot see. When I tap my card, I initiate a cascade of data that travels from a Georgian vending machine to an acquirer in Tbilisi, through international payment networks, to a bank in Poland, and finally to the glowing rectangle in my hand. At each step, the data is transformed, translated, interpreted. At each step, something can go wrong.

Usually, the errors are invisible. A rounding difference here, a fee there. But sometimes the error wears a costume. Sometimes it calls itself "TWN" and expects us to believe that we have been charged in a currency that does not exist, in a country we have never visited, for a coffee we are currently drinking.

I kept the receipt and compared the amounts. If I could not recognize the charge, I would contact my card issuer immediately and follow its advice about securing the card.

The display looked like a typo to me, but only the issuer's transaction records could confirm what happened.

Epilogue

I will contact Erste Bank, of course. I will send them the screenshot, the receipt, perhaps a photograph of my Batumi entry stamp. I will ask them which currency was authorized and settled, why the app displayed TWN, and whether a correction is needed.

But I will also keep the screenshot. I will keep it as a reminder that our financial systems, for all their titanium cards and biometric security and blockchain ambitions, are still fundamentally human constructions. They can be wrong. They can be weird. They can invent currencies that never existed and charge us in them.

And sometimes, if we are paying attention, they can show us exactly how the sausage is made—one phantom transaction at a time.

The coffee, by the way, was excellent. Worth every imaginary Taiwan dollar.
