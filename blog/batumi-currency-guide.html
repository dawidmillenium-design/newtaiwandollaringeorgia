Paying in Batumi: A Traveller's Guide to Georgian Lari (and the Ghost Currencies on Your Statement)

2026-09-29 · Batumi, Georgia · Dawid Lisowski · tags: georgia, batumi, currency, gel, travel-money, dcc

The hook

I bought a coffee for one lari and came home to a bank line in a currency that does not exist. Humid Black Sea air, the colonnaded boulevard, the smell of khachapuri from a bakery on the old town side — and then a statement line reading −1,00 TWN.

1. Ground truth: where you (and your card) actually are

Before you trust a statement line, trust your body and your passport. Money bugs feel like fraud; geography feels like boring paperwork — but geography is the lie detector.

Diagnostic rule #1: a charge is suspicious only if the merchant descriptor and your physical location disagree. When they agree, you have a metadata bug, not a thief.

A cloned card fed through a terminal in Taipei would carry a TW merchant and a TWD amount unrelated to my latte. The stamps, the descriptor, and the greeting all point the same direction: I was in Batumi, paying with my own card, locally.

2. Currency 101 — what you're actually holding in Batumi

Georgia's money is the lari, code GEL, divided into 100 tetri. Tetri sound tiny but they are not optional — markets, marshrutkas, tips and small cafés expect exact-ish change.

  • Card (Visa/Mastercard) works almost everywhere tourists go — hotels, restaurants, supermarkets, pharmacies, ride-apps, parking machines, and the new self-service vending kiosks along Batumi's boulevard. Contactless works almost without fail.
  • Cash still wins at beach-side stalls, old-town bakeries, marshrutka drivers, market vendors, and tips. Carry small lari + a pouch of tetri.
  • ATMs are plentiful; prefer bank-owned machines, and always decline the on-screen "charge me in your home currency" offer (see Trap #1).

Rough orientation (verify live rates before relying on them): 1 GEL ≈ 1.4–1.5 PLN ≈ 0.35–0.40 EUR ≈ 0.35–0.40 USD. Compare two or three exchange bureaus on the main avenue, avoid "no commission" street offers that hide the margin in the rate, and keep receipts if you might re-convert leftover lari at the airport.

3. The three traps that quietly eat your lari

Trap #1 — Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC)

A terminal asks: "Charge you 1.45 zł instead of 1.00 GEL?" That looks helpful and is almost always a worse rate plus a markup. The rule: always pay in the local currency (GEL). Declining DCC is the single highest-ROI habit a traveller can build.

Trap #2 — Informal USD/EUR pricing

Near the beach, on some tours and boat hires, you'll hear prices quoted in dollars or euros. Before you tap, ask explicitly: "GEL or dollars?"

Trap #3 — The ghost code on your statement

Sometimes neither the merchant nor your bank is lying, and the line is still nonsense — because the currency field got corrupted somewhere in the chain terminal → acquirer → network → issuer → app. The amount is right; the three-letter tag is a hallucination. This is exactly what happened to me, and it's the reason this whole post exists.

4. Worked example — the "New Taiwan Dollar" that never was 🇬🇪☕

I tapped for a coffee in Batumi. 1 GEL. The terminal beeped, I walked out thinking about the sea. Later, the banking app showed −1,00 TWN — a currency that does not exist. See the companion post "TWN Is Not a Currency" for the full case file: the amount field survived the trip intact; only the currency tag got mangled between the vending terminal, the Tbilisi-registered acquirer (the city mismatch with my Batumi tap is normal acquirer noise, not a red flag), and my bank's parser.

5. Pre-flight checklist

  • I know whether this vendor quotes GEL or USD/EUR (asked out loud).
  • At the terminal I chose local currency (GEL), declined DCC.
  • I carry small lari + tetri for cash-only spots.
  • I kept the receipt / screenshot for anything over a few lari.
  • If a statement line looks foreign, I checked merchant descriptor + my location before assuming fraud.
  • I verified the live FX rate today rather than trusting a remembered number.
  • Leftover lari: I either spent the tetri or kept the exchange receipt.

6. Batumi-specific notes

  • The boulevard & beach strip is the most card-friendly part of the city; the old town backstreets and the botanical-garden approach cafés are where cash suddenly matters.
  • Parking meters and vending kiosks are card-first and exactly where weird statement metadata tends to surface — cheap terminals with thin firmware, often operated by Tbilisi-registered processors, so the descriptor city won't match where you stand. Don't let that spook you; check the amount.
  • Marshrutkas to nearby towns pay cash, small notes, no change for big bills.
  • Tips: round up or leave ~10% in cash at sit-down places; card tips aren't always passed on.
  • Beach-side umbrellas/loungers frequently want cash — agree the number before you sit.

Close

We hand our trust to a chain of invisible boxes — terminal, acquirer, network, issuer, app — each one re-typing what the last said. The lari was real, the coffee was real, the stamps were real; only the three letters were a ghost. So the next time your statement shows a currency you've never heard of, breathe, look at your passport, look at the amount, and then decide whether you have a thief or just a machine that forgot what world it lives in.
